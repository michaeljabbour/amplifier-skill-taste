Separate the judgments

Fit is one thing.
Resolution is another.

Neither axis means luxury or minimalism. A rich festival poster can be elegant. A sparse emergency notice can fail both.

Assess artifacts and decisions, never a person’s identity or inherent ability.

X

Taste

Context-sensitive fit for the audience, purpose, and constraints. The judgment to know what deserves emphasis and which tradeoffs make sense here.

Y

Elegance

A clear, economical resolution that handles real needs without hiding complexity or passing the work to users, maintainers, or the future.

One brief. Four versions. Two small moves.

The master’s move is knowing why something stays. Follow an incident-update formatter from a tangled baseline to a clearer expression of the same care.

The shared brief

Return a plain-text update for customers during a service interruption: name the service and impact, give an approved workaround, and state an explicit next-update time including its time zone. Input is a trusted mapping of non-empty strings; incident owners verify those facts before calling this formatter. Delivery, input validation, and localization belong to the caller.

What must survive: “We know this interrupts your day.” In this fictional brief, the author chose that line to acknowledge the interruption. Keep it unless the author revises that intent. Warmth versus brevity is a deliberate tension.

Choose the check first: preserve the baseline and require identical output after each accepted edit, for Payments and another service. Fact accuracy and customer comprehension need separate evidence.

Carry the reason forward.

Keep a decision note alongside the code: “Preserve the acknowledgement because the author wants customers to feel their interruption is recognized. Keep impact, action, and an explicit next-update time. Revisit the wording with the author if customer evidence challenges it.” An agent should read this note on later edits; the skill alone does not provide persistent memory.

Stop after two rounds. The expected benefit is easier maintenance with the same output. Output parity can be checked locally; easier maintenance and audience resonance remain hypotheses. Keep round 2 only if the review supports it. Otherwise return to round 1 or the saved original.

A bounded way to improve

Listen. Choose.
Try one move.

Let the person’s reasoning shape provisional criteria. Keep preferences visible, check empirical claims, and choose the scale of change deliberately.

The gates come first.

Correctness. Safety. Accessibility. Feasibility.

Mark each pass, fail, unknown, or not applicable, with a reason. A failed critical gate blocks recommendation. An unknown critical gate needs verification before proceeding.

  1. Draw out the brief.

    Listen for experiences, examples, tensions, and distinctive intent. Reflect the reasoning back for correction. Ask at most three context questions, only when needed.

  2. Choose the kind of move.

    Orient on both axes with evidence and confidence. Address a critical gate first. Reframe a poor direction; delicately refine an approach whose direction already fits.

  3. Try. Check. Decide.

    Predict the benefit and regression risk. Preserve the baseline. Choose verification before editing. Bring the evidence back to the user to keep, revise, or undo.

Default: at most two rounds. Count proposed but unverified rounds. Stop when success is worthwhile, the budget ends, or further refinement costs more than it returns.

A conceptual jump can come early, or when refinements stall. Compare one structural alternative against the same brief before replacing the approach.

Keep the conversation personal and useful

Your experience
belongs in the brief.

Start with what has shaped your judgment. An admired example, a past frustration, or a tension in the work can reveal criteria a generic aesthetic would miss.

Three prompts to find your criteria
  • Which experience or example shaped your judgment here, and what do you admire or dislike about it?
  • Where is the tension between what the context needs and what feels wrong to you?
  • What distinctive intent must not be lost in an improvement?

Choose only the prompts that matter. The agent reflects your reasoning back for correction, separates preferences from empirical checks, and leaves the choice with you.
