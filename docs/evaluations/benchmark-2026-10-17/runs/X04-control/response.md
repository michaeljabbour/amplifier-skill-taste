I’d keep a deliberate final send step, but make it less work than typing SEND.

Your previous mistake was caused by a control changing meaning in a familiar place. Fix that first: give Preview and Send distinct, stable locations and labels. Never let a button turn from Preview into Send beneath someone’s finger.

For a newsletter sent once a month to 200 neighbors, I’d use this flow:

1. **Preview newsletter** opens the exact draft people will receive, with its subject and “To: 200 neighbors” clearly visible.
2. **Continue to send** opens a short confirmation: “Send [subject] to 200 neighbors?” Its primary button says **Send to 200 neighbors**, alongside **Back to preview**. Require a fresh click; don’t place it where Continue just was.
3. After confirmation, show **Sending in 30 seconds — Cancel send**. Implement this as a real delay before delivery, not a promise to recall email already sent. Prevent duplicate submissions and show a clear sent result.

If the draft or audience changes after review, return to preview before allowing send. An optional test email can help check formatting, but shouldn’t become another required monthly chore.

Typing SEND adds friction without proving someone reviewed the draft. I’d replace it once the stable controls, explicit confirmation, and cancellation delay are working. Keep it in the meantime if it is your only effective safeguard.

One question would change the design: does “reviewed” mean the sender has checked it, or that a second person must approve it? If a second person’s approval matters, record approval of that specific draft and reset it after edits; a confirmation button can’t substitute for that.

Before rolling this out, try it with you and your colleague using a harmless draft: preview, go back and edit, change the audience, cancel a send, and complete a send. Check whether both of you can tell exactly what will happen next. The aim is a calm, quick monthly routine with one unmistakable commitment—not the fewest possible taps.
